Understanding Schrödinger's Equation

Published on April 12, 2025 by Nadiantara

Tags: quantum mechanics physics mathematics

The Schrödinger equation is a fundamental equation in quantum mechanics that describes how the quantum state of a physical system changes over time. In its most general form, it’s written as:

$$ i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi(\mathbf{r},t) = \hat{H}\Psi(\mathbf{r},t) $$

where:

  • $i$ is the imaginary unit
  • $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant
  • $\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)$ is the wave function
  • $\hat{H}$ is the Hamiltonian operator (total energy)

The Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

For systems where the Hamiltonian is time-independent, we can use the time-independent Schrödinger equation:

$$ \hat{H}\Psi(\mathbf{r}) = E\Psi(\mathbf{r}) $$

This is an eigenvalue equation where $E$ represents the energy of the system, and the wavefunctions $\Psi$ are eigenstates of the Hamiltonian.

Physical Meaning

The wave function $\Psi$ contains all the information about a quantum system. According to the Born interpretation, $|\Psi(\mathbf{r},t)|^2$ gives the probability density of finding a particle at position $\mathbf{r}$ at time $t$.

Applications

The Schrödinger equation has been successfully applied to:

  1. The hydrogen atom
  2. Quantum harmonic oscillator
  3. Quantum tunneling
  4. Chemical bonding

Historical Significance

Erwin Schrödinger proposed this equation in 1925, providing a wave mechanics formulation of quantum theory. Along with Heisenberg’s matrix mechanics (which was shown to be mathematically equivalent), it revolutionized our understanding of atomic and subatomic phenomena.

The equation elegantly captures the wave-particle duality that is central to quantum mechanics, and its solutions have explained numerous experimental observations that classical physics could not account for.
